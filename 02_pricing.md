# Pricing — From Zero

**For:** William Parker · Parker AI Works
**Drafted:** 2026-05-11

Your first realistic customers are (a) someone you already know and (b) online/nationwide remote. That second one matters a lot. It means you are **not** pricing for the East Carroll Parish economy. You are pricing for the national AI-consulting market while *living* in a cheap parish. That's the whole geographic arbitrage and it's a real advantage — keep it in mind every time someone tries to push you under $1,000 on a real project.

This doc covers: how to think about price, what the market actually pays, what *you* should charge given no portfolio yet, how to raise rates, when to refuse hourly, and how to stop scope creep from eating you alive.

---

## 1. Three ways anyone sets a price

There are only three, no matter what industry:

1. **Cost-plus** — "It costs me $400 of my time, so I charge $600." Used by manufacturing and trades. Useless for you. Your actual cost is negligible (a Claude subscription and electricity). If you cost-plus this, you'll charge $50.

2. **Market** — "Other people doing similar work charge X, so I charge near X." Most consultants live here. It's safe and lazy. It also means you cap yourself at the average.

3. **Value-based** — "This will save the client 10 hours a week × $50/hr loaded cost × 52 weeks = $26,000/year. I charge $5,000." Gold standard. Requires you to (a) understand the client's business and (b) have enough confidence to make the math out loud. You'll get to this in 6–12 months. Not on day one.

**For now:** price at market with a deliberate beginner discount. As case studies pile up, shift toward value-based.

## 2. What the AI consulting market actually pays in 2026

Rough buckets, US:

| Segment | What they sell | Typical price |
|---|---|---|
| **Fiverr / Upwork "prompt seller"** | One-off prompts, GPTs | $50–$500 per gig |
| **Solo AI consultant, no track record** | Audits, basic setups | $500–$2,500 per project |
| **Solo AI consultant, 5+ case studies** | Workflow builds, training | $3,000–$10,000 per project |
| **Boutique AI agency (3–10 people)** | Workflow + integrations | $10,000–$50,000 per project; $5–15k/mo retainer |
| **Senior contractor at established firm** | Hourly | $200–$500/hr |
| **In-house Bay Area prompt engineer FTE** | Salary equivalent | $15,000–$30,000/mo loaded |

You are at the top of bucket two. Your strategy-doc prices put you there exactly on purpose: above tire-kicker territory, below "established consultant" rates that nobody will believe yet.

**The catch:** most of the people in bucket 2 charge those prices because they think they're worth it, not because they are. About a third of them deserve bucket 3 money and don't realize. The other two-thirds shouldn't be charging at all. Your job is to actually deliver well so you climb to bucket 3 inside 12 months.

## 3. The "first ten clients" rule

This is the most important pricing concept on this page.

**Clients 1–3** are not for revenue. They are for proof. Charge low — even 50% off list — but only in exchange for a written testimonial and permission to anonymize the work as a case study. Without case studies you cannot move up the ladder. With three good ones, you can.

**Clients 4–7:** Charge list price ($750 / $2,500 / $1,500 / $2,000-mo). No exceptions. Anyone who says "but your last client paid less" hears: "they took a founder-discount in exchange for being case studies. That program is closed."

**Clients 8–10:** Raise prices 20% per new client until somebody pushes back hard. The first person who says "that's too much" and walks away — *that's your market price.* Drop back one step from there.

This isn't theoretical. It's how every solo consultant who's ever been honest has described their first year.

## 5. Hourly is a trap. When to refuse it.

Hourly rates do three bad things:

1. **Cap your income** at hours-in-a-day × rate. You can never out-earn time.
2. **Punish efficiency.** Get faster at your job and you make less money for the same outcome.
3. **Train clients to count hours,** which means every minute becomes a justification conversation.

**Refuse hourly when** the scope is buildable as a fixed-fee project. Which is most things.

**You can name an hourly rate** for two purposes only:
- **Change orders.** "Anything outside the original SOW bills at $200/hr in 30-minute increments." This is a deterrent more than a billing model. It exists so clients self-regulate scope creep.
- **Ad-hoc consultation.** Some clients want to "pick your brain" for an hour. Charge them $200 (after the free 20-minute discovery call). It filters tire-kickers and gives you a fallback when nothing else fits.

Your hourly rate, on paper, is **$200/hr**. You will rarely use it. That's the point — it exists so fixed-fee projects look like a bargain by comparison.

## 6. Scope creep — the #1 way solo consultants lose money

Scope creep is when a $2,500 Workflow Pack quietly turns into $6,000 of work because the client kept adding "one more small thing."

Three rules:

### Rule 1: Write down "done" before you start

Every SOW (Statement of Work) has a **Deliverables** section that lists, by bullet point, exactly what you will hand over. Example for a Workflow Pack:

> **Deliverables**
> - Three documented prompts for the [chosen workflow], delivered as a Claude Project or Custom GPT
> - One PDF user guide (≤ 5 pages) covering how to run each prompt
> - One 60-minute training session for up to 4 team members, recorded
> - 30 days of fix-it support after delivery

That's it. Anything not on that list is not in scope.

### Rule 2: Change orders, not favors

When a client asks for "just one more thing," the script is:

> "Happy to add that. Quick note — that's outside the original scope, so I'll send a change order with the price and timeline. Once you sign it, I'll start on it."

Then you send a one-page change order ($X for Y work, completed by Z date). They sign. You work. You bill.

The first time you do this it feels rude. It is not rude. It is the second-most professional thing you can do for the client — the most professional is *delivering what you promised.*

### Rule 3: The "fix-it window" is not "free new work"

Your 30-day fix-it window covers **bugs in what you delivered.** Not new ideas. If a prompt doesn't work the way the SOW described, you fix it free. If the client decides they want a different workflow now, that's a new project, new price.

## 7. Common pricing mistakes — don't make these

- **"Let me know your budget"** — never. You name the price first. Once they say a number, you cannot unhear it, and they cannot unhear the lower of (their number, your number).
- **Discounting to close a deal.** If someone won't pay $2,500 for a Workflow Pack, they will be the worst client you've ever had at $1,500. The kind of client who pays full freight is the kind of client who respects the work. Discounted clients haggle forever.
- **One option only.** Always offer two or three. "Audit at $750, Pack at $2,500, or both bundled at $3,000." Anchoring effect: the middle option becomes the default.
- **Pricing on email.** Always price on a call, after you've heard the problem. Email is for sending the proposal *after* the call.
- **Charging less because you live in a cheap parish.** Your client doesn't live there. They are paying for outcome, not your zip code. Charge what the work is worth.

## 8. Working with someone you know — the awkward part

You said your first realistic customer might be someone you already know. Real talk: those engagements end relationships more often than they don't, *unless* you treat them as professionally as a stranger from day one.

The friction points and how to handle them:

1. **They expect a discount you didn't offer.** Pre-empt it. The moment they say "I want to hire you," you say: "Great — and because you're a friend, I want to make sure this is clean. I have three founder-discount slots at 50% off list price. They come with a testimonial requirement and an anonymized case study. Want one?" Now the discount is a structured offer, not a favor. They can take it or pay full.

2. **They text you at 9pm asking for tweaks.** This happens with strangers too but worse with friends. Set communication rules in the SOW: "All requests via email or Slack. Response within one business day. Off-hours work bills at the change-order rate." Then enforce it on day one. The first 9pm text gets a Tuesday-morning reply.

3. **They overshare what they want and underpay for it.** Friends will assume their idea is small and your work is easy. Write the deliverables list in the SOW, get them to sign it, and refer to it constantly. "Per the SOW, we're building three prompts for the proposal workflow. What you're asking about now is a fourth workflow — let me send a change order."

4. **The work fails and they're awkward about it.** The 30-day fix-it window and the half-fee refund clause both apply. Use them. Refunding a friend's project that didn't land is way cheaper than the resentment of pretending it did.

## 10. TL;DR — what to actually do this week

1. Pick **two** people you know who have a small business and might pay $375 for an AI audit. Just two.
2. Send them a one-paragraph email: "I'm launching an AI consulting practice. I have three founder slots at 50% off in exchange for a testimonial. Interested?"

4. Do the work. Get the testimonial. Anonymize the case study.
5. Repeat for client 2 and 3. Then close the founder slots.
6. List price after that. No exceptions.

If you can't find two people willing to pay $375 each for an AI audit, the business isn't broken — the offer or the pitch is. We'll fix the pitch, not the price.
